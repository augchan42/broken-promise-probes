# Linear probes vs SAEs (Qwen-Scope): a primer

Status: current to 9 November 2026. Written from the research notes in `research_notes/Linear probes vs SAEs/`.


For reading a known concept out of the residual stream, a supervised linear probe on raw activations matches or beats a probe built on SAE features in every controlled comparison found up to November 2026. The gap is small in distribution and larger under distribution shift. On 113 datasets, SAE probes were the best method on 14 and did not improve ensembles ([arXiv 2502.16681](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16681)). On harmful-intent detection, a dense probe reached 0.999 AUROC out of distribution, and probing through the SAE reconstruction lost 0.048 ([Alignment Forum, 26 Mar 2025](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/4uXCAJNuPKtKBsi28/sae-progress-update-2-draft)). SAEs are useful for a different job: finding concepts you did not name in advance, debugging datasets, auditing, and explaining what a probe picked up. Neither method is a reliable steering tool. Prompting beats both on AxBench, and difference-of-means beats SAE latents ([arXiv 2501.17148](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17148)). Neither survives being used as a frozen training target. For this repo, the plain activation probe on Qwen3.5-27B should be the main broken-promise detector. The Qwen-Scope SAEs for that model have 81,920 latents per layer on all 64 layers. They were trained on the post-trained checkpoint, which is the model the repo probes. Their job here is to show whether the probe direction lines up with betrayal-topic features or with something closer to intent. That question also needs a behavioural control: test the probe within ally promises and within rival promises separately.

## Both read the residual stream; the difference is who picks the concept

With a linear probe, you choose the concept and supply labels. With an SAE, the training objective decides what the dictionary contains, and you interpret the latents afterwards.

### Linear probes, as taught in ARENA 1.3.1

You pick a concept, such as true vs. false or honest vs. deceptive. You build a labelled dataset, cache activations at one layer, and fit one direction. The ARENA lab "[1.3.1] Linear Probes" (ARENA_3.0, chapter 1, page last regenerated 4 Nov 2026) has five sections ([ARENA lab](https://github.com/callummcdougall/ARENA_3.0/blob/main/chapter1_transformer_interp/instructions/pages/11_%5B1.3.1%5D_Linear_Probes.md)). Section 1 covers activation extraction, PCA and a layer sweep. Section 2 fits difference-of-means (MM) and logistic-regression (LR) probes on **Llama-2-13B (base) at layer 14**, using the Geometry of Truth datasets `cities`, `sp_en_trans` and `larger_than`. It adds a 3×3 cross-dataset transfer matrix, cosine similarity between probe directions, and a discussion of CCS (implementing CCS is a bonus). Section 3 adds or subtracts the truth direction at layers 8 to 14 and measures the effect on P(TRUE) − P(FALSE). Section 4 trains deception probes on **Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct** with Apollo's instructed-pairs method (512 true facts, honest vs. dishonest system prompt). The default layer is 16 of 32. A bonus averages scores over the middle 50% of layers, and the section also loads Apollo's pre-trained probe for Llama-3.3-70B (layer 22 of 80). Section 5 trains attention probes that pool over all token positions, for **high-stakes detection** on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct at layer 16. That target is a property of the user's input, not of model intent. Section 5 is a reduced replication: the McKenzie et al. paper it follows used Llama-3.3-70B at layer 31 ([arXiv 2506.10805](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10805)). The lab uses Hugging Face `transformers` with forward hooks, not TransformerLens.

Two implementation details matter if you copy the lab. The MM probe needs the midpoint bias: the lab probes uncentred activations, and it says dropping the bias costs about 20 points of accuracy. The LR probe is sklearn `LogisticRegression(fit_intercept=False)` on standardised activations, with C=0.1 for truth and C=0.001 for deception. Training cost is minutes on cached activations.

### SAEs, and Qwen-Scope

An SAE learns a dictionary of sparse latents without labels. It encodes an activation x as f(x) = σ(W_enc x + b_enc) and reconstructs it as x̂ = W_dec f(x) + b_dec, with few non-zero entries in f ([Gemma Scope 2 technical paper, 16 Sep 2025](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/DeepMind.com/Blog/gemma-scope-2-helping-the-ai-safety-community-deepen-understanding-of-complex-language-model-behavior/Gemma_Scope_2_Technical_Paper.pdf)). Each latent has an encoder row (which inputs turn it on) and a decoder column (what it writes back). Gemma Scope 2 calls these "latents" to separate them from the hypothetical true "features" of the model.

Qwen-Scope is the Qwen team's open SAE release: **14 SAE groups over 7 Qwen3 and Qwen3.5 backbones**, dense and MoE. All are residual-stream SAEs (`resid_post`, the output of each decoder layer) on every layer, with TopK sparsity at k = 50 or 100 ([arXiv 2605.11887](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11887); [HF collection](https://huggingface.co/collections/Qwen/qwen-scope)). Widths are **16× the hidden size**, except the two wide MoE groups, which are 128K (131,072, or 64×). Every backbone used a Base checkpoint except **Qwen3.5-27B, whose SAEs were trained on the instruct (post-trained) checkpoint** ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11887v1)). The Qwen team paid the training cost. You download the weights and look up which latents fire.

## How each is built in 2026

### Probe families: the classifier and the steering direction differ

On simple concepts, LR, MM, LDA and LAT (PCA of contrast differences) reach similar AUROC. Apollo found LR rejected control data (Alpaca) best, and SAE-feature probes did worse than raw-activation probes ([arXiv 2502.03407](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.03407)). For writing a concept back into the model, MM wins consistently. Geometry of Truth says "simple difference-in-mean probes generalize as well as other probing techniques while identifying directions which are more causally implicated in model outputs" ([arXiv 2310.06824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06824)). Im & Li prove that under a pointwise squared-error objective the optimal steering vector is the mean of differences. In their experiments it "consistently outperforms other methods by a large margin", and PCA-based (RepE-style) directions do worst. The top PCA direction is "nearly orthogonal to the steering vector" ([arXiv 2502.02716](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.02716), Feb 2025). The refusal direction is the standard example of a causal MM direction. It is one difference-of-means vector per model, tested on 13 chat models up to 72B. Erasing it stops refusal, and adding it causes refusal on harmless prompts ([arXiv 2406.11717](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717), Jun 2024).

One explanation fits these results, but it is the notes' synthesis, not a claim from a single paper. A regularised LR or LDA direction is roughly Σ⁻¹(μ₊ − μ₋). It down-weights high-variance nuisance directions and can use features that correlate with the label without the model writing them. Steering needs the direction the model reads downstream, which on average is the raw mean difference. CCS, the unsupervised alternative, is mostly discredited for this purpose. Arbitrary features satisfy its consistency structure, and it finds "whatever feature of the activations is most prominent" ([arXiv 2312.10029](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10029), Dec 2023). PCA on contrast pairs recovers about 97% of CCS accuracy ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/9vwekjD6xyuePX7Zr/contrast-pairs-drive-the-empirical-performance-of-contrast), via the ARENA summary [unverified]).

### Aggregation is the main probe change since 2025

The linear map has not changed much since 2025. What changed is how per-token scores are pooled into one decision. McKenzie et al. compared mean, max, last-token, max-of-rolling-means, softmax and attention probes on Llama-3.3-70B. Attention had the highest dev AUROC (>0.95), and last-token "consistently performs poorly". The final probes reach mean AUROC >0.91 on out-of-distribution sets at about six orders of magnitude less compute than fine-tuned 8B to 12B monitors. But the best probe "detects only 43% of high-stakes cases at 1% FPR, compared to 55% for the best LLM baseline" ([arXiv 2506.10805](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10805), Jun 2025, NeurIPS 2025). In Kantamneni et al., adding an attention-pooled baseline cut the SAE probes' win rate from 19.6% to 8.7% ([arXiv 2502.16681](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16681)). Multi-layer ensembles also help. Deception directions rotate across layers, and ensembling raised AUROC by 29% on Insider Trading ([arXiv 2604.13386](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.13386), Apr 2026; see [deception_probes.md](../../research_notes/Broken%20promise%20probes%20related%20work/deception_probes.md)).

### Production probes are cheap first stages in cascades

Two labs have published probes in production, and both use them for misuse, not deception. Google DeepMind probed Gemini 2.5 Flash for cyber-offensive misuse ([arXiv 2601.11516](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11516), Jan 2026). Long context broke the older probes. On long-context attacks, the false-negative rate was **99.13% for a linear mean-pooled probe** and 87.88% for an attention probe. The selected "Max of Rolling Means Attention" probe (10 heads, window 10) had 3.03% FNR at 1.23% FPR. Its test error was 2.64%, against 6.18% for linear mean and 2.04% for prompted Gemini 2.5 Flash. A probe-then-Flash cascade that defers about 8% of inputs beat Flash alone at about 1/50 of the cost, and the results "informed the successful deployment of misuse mitigation probes in user-facing instances of Gemini". Anthropic's representation re-use work found that an EMA probe (per-token scores, EMA α=0.5, take the max) beat a dedicated classifier with about 2% of the policy's parameters. A mean-token probe was worse than the smallest dedicated classifier ([Anthropic Alignment blog, 2025](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/cheap-monitors/)). Constitutional Classifiers++ ensembles "efficient linear probe classifiers" with external classifiers. It reports 40× lower compute than the baseline exchange classifier and a 0.05% refusal rate on production traffic ([arXiv 2601.04603](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04603), Jan 2026). Whether its probes screen all traffic and escalate is [unverified]. In every deployed system found, the probe is a cheap filter in front of a stronger model, never a standalone detector. Recall at 1% FPR is the operating metric. AUROC alone hides a weak low-FPR tail.

### SAE architectures moved from L1 to TopK, JumpReLU and Matryoshka

The original recipe was ReLU plus an L1 penalty ([Towards Monosemanticity, Oct 2023](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2023/monosemantic-features); [arXiv 2309.08600](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08600)). Anthropic scaled it to 34M latents on Claude 3 Sonnet ([Scaling Monosemanticity, May 2024](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/)) [not re-checked]. L1 shrinks activation magnitudes, so later designs separate "which latents fire" from "how large". Gated SAEs use one path for gating and another for magnitude ([arXiv 2404.16014](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16014), Apr 2024) [not re-checked]. **TopK** keeps the k largest pre-activations and zeroes the rest. It gives direct control of L0 and scaled to a 16M-latent SAE on GPT-4. It also introduced the normalised-MSE (FVU) metric and an auxiliary loss against dead latents ([arXiv 2406.04093](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04093), Jun 2024) [not re-checked]. **JumpReLU** uses a learned threshold per latent and is trained with an L0 penalty through straight-through estimators ([arXiv 2407.14435](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14435), Jul 2024). **BatchTopK** applies the top-k constraint over a batch, so the number of active latents varies per token ([arXiv 2412.06410](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06410), Dec 2024). **Matryoshka** SAEs train nested prefixes of the dictionary to reconstruct on their own, so early latents learn general concepts and later ones specifics ([arXiv 2503.17547](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.17547v1), Mar 2025). A 2026 survey reports first-letter absorption on Gemma-2-2B falling from 0.49 to 0.05 at matched sparsity [verified, secondary] ([arXiv 2609.06862](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.06862)).

**Gemma Scope 2** is the reference recipe in late 2026 ([technical paper, 16 Sep 2025](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/DeepMind.com/Blog/gemma-scope-2-helping-the-ai-safety-community-deepen-understanding-of-complex-language-model-behavior/Gemma_Scope_2_Technical_Paper.pdf); public coverage 12 Jan 2026, [InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/01/google-gemma-scope-2/)). It uses JumpReLU with a quadratic L0 penalty and a second penalty on high-frequency latents, which can remove all latents that fire on more than 10% of tokens. It uses Matryoshka training and untied encoder and decoder. Instruct-model SAEs are initialised from the pretrained-model SAEs and fine-tuned on chat rollouts. Selected SAEs get a KL fine-tune "with the goal of learning latents which are functionally important for the model's predictions rather than just for reconstructing activations". It covers Gemma 3 from 270M to 27B, PT and IT, on all layers and sublayers. Widths run 16k to 1M, and the card recommends 64k or 256k at "medium" L0 (30 to 60) ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-scope-2-4b-it)). Choosing L0 is still convention, not a principled rule. GS2's own plots show higher L0 and wider SAEs give better reconstruction. Residual-stream SAEs have the highest increase in LM loss when spliced in, even though FVU is similar across sites.

### Successors model computation, not one site

**Transcoders** approximate an MLP's output from its input ([arXiv 2406.11944](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11944), Jun 2024). Skip transcoders add an affine skip connection, and GS2 confirms this improves FVU against L0 ([arXiv 2501.18823](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18823), Jan 2025). **Crosscoders** read and write concatenated activations from several layers or several models, which supports model diffing ([Anthropic, Oct 2024](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/crosscoders/index.html)). L1 crosscoders mislabel many latents as chat-only, while BatchTopK crosscoders mostly do not ([arXiv 2504.02922](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.02922), Apr 2025). **Cross-layer transcoders (CLTs)** read from one layer's residual stream and write to all later MLP outputs. Anthropic uses them to build a replacement model and per-prompt attribution graphs ([Circuit Tracing, Mar 2025](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/methods.html); [Biology, Mar 2025](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/biology.html)). Anthropic open-sourced circuit-tracer on 29 May 2025, with a Neuronpedia frontend ([Anthropic](https://anthropic.com/research/open-source-circuit-tracing)). GS2 ships CLTs for 270M and 1B and replicates the finding that CLT graphs are sparser than per-layer transcoder graphs. Anthropic's May 2026 update argues that a feature's downstream connections, not its top activations, predict whether it will steer behaviour ([Transformer Circuits, May 2026](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/may-update/index.html)). Weight-space methods decompose parameters instead of activations: APD ([arXiv 2501.14926](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14926)) and SPD ([arXiv 2506.20790](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.20790v2)). These remain at toy or small-model scale. Qwen-Scope has none of these successors. It ships residual-stream SAEs only.

The paper's quantitative results all use Qwen3 models (1.7B, 8B, 30B-A3B). **None uses the Qwen3.5-27B SAEs.** The results: feature-based benchmark redundancy correlates with performance-based redundancy at Spearman ρ ≈ 0.85 across 17 benchmarks and 26 checkpoints, and 63% of GSM8K's features are covered by MATH. Toxicity classification has English F1 above 0.90 on Qwen3-1.7B and 8B. Feature-driven safety data synthesis reaches 99.74% target-feature coverage on Qwen3-8B. A feature regulariser during SFT (SASFT) cuts the code-switch ratio on Qwen3-1.7B-Base by 72% (zh), 85% (ru) and 100% (ko), but on Qwen3-8B-Base zh only by 31%. The "over 50%" figure in press coverage matches only the 1.7B rows. Steering with a repetition feature during RL raised MGSM from 77.64 to 82.40 on Qwen3-30B-A3B but lowered IFEval on Qwen3-8B (70.42 to 68.96). The authors call these downstream effects "mixed and task-dependent". The steering examples, suppressing a Chinese-language latent and pushing toward Classical Chinese, are qualitative only ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11887v1)).

Independent work on Qwen-Scope is small. Riegler & Torpmann-Hagen steered with the Qwen3-1.7B SAE at layer 20 ([arXiv 2605.03160](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.03160v1), 4 May 2026). An "AI self-disclaimer" latent was non-monotonic: disclaimers fell from 87.5% at baseline to 0% at c=+1000, and c=+500 produced a "contemplative-philosopher voice". The authors note that base-trained SAEs were applied to post-trained activations. A concurrent Alibaba paper contrasts design choices but runs no experiments on Qwen-Scope weights ([arXiv 2606.26620](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26620v1)). The notes found no independent reconstruction benchmark.

## The comparison, corrected

| | Linear probe | SAE (e.g. Qwen-Scope) |
|---|---|---|
| Supervision | Labels needed | None at training; latents labelled afterwards from top activations or an auto-interp pipeline |
| What you get | One direction for one concept you name in advance | Tens of thousands of latents per layer (81,920 for Qwen3.5-27B), including concepts you did not think to look for |
| Objective | Classification loss (LR) or class-mean difference (MM) | Reconstruction under a sparsity constraint (TopK k=50 or 100 for Qwen-Scope) |
| Detection, known concept | Usually higher. Small in-distribution gap (about 0.002 to 0.03 AUROC), larger out of distribution (about 0.04 to 0.05) | Usually lower. The concept can be split across latents, absorbed into child latents, or mixed with others |
| Discovery | None. It answers only the question you ask | Main strength: auditing, dataset debugging, hypothesis generation |
| Steering | Add or patch the direction (lab §3). MM steers well, the LR direction poorly | Clamp or remove a latent through its decoder column. Below MM on AxBench |
| Cost to you | Minutes on cached activations, plus the cost of a labelled dataset | Free if a suite exists. For 27B, about 3.4 GB per layer in fp32. Training your own is very expensive |
| Model coverage | Any model, any layer | Only released suites. Qwen-Scope covers 7 Qwen backbones, residual stream only |
| Reported quality | AUROC, recall at fixed FPR, cross-dataset transfer | Qwen-Scope reports no FVU or delta loss. Other suites report FVU and delta LM loss |
| Failure modes | Fits spurious correlates and context. Mean pooling fails at long context. Aliased labels | Dead, dense, split or absorbed latents. Seed-dependent. Labels can mislead about function. Reconstruction error hides part of the computation |

Detection numbers come from the benchmark section below. Steering numbers come from AxBench ([arXiv 2501.17148](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17148v3)).

## Benchmarks: SAE probes lose detection by small margins, more out of distribution

The best-controlled study is Kantamneni, Engels, Rajamanoharan, Tegmark and Nanda ([arXiv 2502.16681](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16681), 23 Feb 2025, ICML 2025). It used 113 binary datasets on Gemma-2-9B with Gemma Scope SAEs at 16k, 131k and 1M, and replicated on Llama-3.1-8B with Llama Scope. Baselines were logistic regression, PCA regression, KNN, XGBoost and an MLP. SAE probes were best on **14 of 113 tasks**, and adding them to the toolkit slightly lowered performance. They gave no average improvement under data scarcity, class imbalance or label noise, and baselines won under covariate shift. Eight SAE architectures from Oct 2023 to Dec 2024 showed only a slight, non-significant upward trend. No SAE-plus-baseline ensemble consistently beat baseline-only ensembles.

GDM's mechanistic interpretability team ran a harmful-intent test on Gemma-2-9B-IT at layer 20, with 4,046 training, 1,014 validation and 3,850 out-of-distribution examples ([Alignment Forum, 26 Mar 2025](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/4uXCAJNuPKtKBsi28/sae-progress-update-2-draft)). The dense probe scored AUROC 1.0 train, 1.0 val and **0.999 OOD**. Single-latent SAE probes could not fit the training data. Probes on about 20 SAE latents fit and generalised in distribution but transferred worse OOD. The exact OOD AUROC is only in figures. Probing the SAE reconstruction instead of the raw residual lost 0.00087 AUROC on train, 0.0018 on test and **0.048 OOD**. Fine-tuning the SAE on chat data closed about half the gap (0.039). The team then said it was "deprioritising fundamental SAE research for the moment". That is narrower than often reported. It covered SAE architecture research, not SAE use, and GDM released Gemma Scope 2 later that year.

AxBench measured detection over 500 concepts on Gemma-2-2B and 9B ([arXiv 2501.17148](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17148v3), Jan 2025, ICML 2025). Mean AUROC: DiffMean 0.942, linear probe 0.940, ReFT-r1 0.938, prompting 0.929, SAE with latents chosen using labels 0.917, and **SAE with the latent chosen by its auto-interp label 0.695**. With supervised latent selection, SAEs are about 0.025 below DiffMean. When you pick the latent by its description, as a user browsing Neuronpedia would, they are about 0.25 below. Apollo's deception work found the same direction on Llama-3.3-70B ([arXiv 2502.03407](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.03407)). The Among Us study is often cited here but is not a controlled comparison ([arXiv 2504.04072](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04072)). Its SAE features (Goodfire, Llama-3.3-70B) ranged from 0.967 AUROC for the best honesty feature down to 0.182 for a "contexts involving deception/lying" feature, while its linear probes ran on Phi-4 (0.94 to 0.99). That 0.182 feature is a direct example of a latent that tracks deception being discussed, not deception being done.

The one reported SAE win is commercial. Goodfire and Rakuten report that SAE probes "won out" when moving a PII guardrail from synthetic to real data, at 15 to 500× lower cost than an LLM judge ([Goodfire](https://www.goodfire.ai/customer-stories/rakuten)) [snippet]. A 96% F1 figure is [unverified] ([StartupHub](https://www.startuphub.ai/news/rakuten-deploys-new-guardrail-for-sae-pii-detection)). There is no public head-to-head table, so treat it as a claim. A Jan 2026 board-state preprint reports residual-stream linear probes "decisively outperform" SAE probes [snippet] ([Preprints.org](https://www.preprints.org/frontend/manuscript/940b242abb6b3fe2846c1b9d844699d4/download_pub)).

**SAEBench** compares SAEs with each other, not with dense probes ([arXiv 2503.09532](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09532v4), Mar 2025, ICML 2025). It runs 200+ SAEs over 8 metrics: core (loss recovered and L0), auto-interp, sparse probing, RAVEL, absorption, unlearning, spurious correlation removal (SCR) and targeted probe perturbation (TPP). Proxy metrics did not predict downstream performance, and metric rankings disagreed. Matryoshka BatchTopK was weaker on the sparsity-fidelity frontier but best on absorption, RAVEL, sparse probing and SCR. Its sparse-probing baseline is K raw neurons, not a full dense probe. SAEs trained on a randomly initialised model scored "close to or worse than" that baseline. Chanin's May 2026 audit found that **TPP and SCR "should not be used to evaluate SAEs"** because they fail reseed-noise and ground-truth tests. The other metrics were noisier than assumed, and k-sparse probing was the most reliable ([arXiv 2605.18229](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18229), 18 May 2026). Do not cite SAEBench SCR or TPP scores as evidence of usefulness without that caveat.

## Steering: prompting first, difference-of-means beats SAE latents

AxBench's steering scores are judged by an LLM on concept, instruction relevance and fluency, combined as a harmonic mean from 0 to 2 ([arXiv 2501.17148](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17148v3)). Prompting scored 0.894, LoReFT 0.741, SFT 0.676, LoRA 0.615, ReFT-r1 0.543, **DiffMean 0.239, SAE 0.165**, LAT 0.127, PCA 0.105 and **linear probe 0.098**. The linear probe is second best at detection and last at steering. That is the detection/steering split from ARENA §3 at benchmark scale. Jørgensen & Hansen claim that supervised latent selection and labelling bring SAE steering "close to on par with the reference LoRA performance", still below prompting ([arXiv 2605.31183](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31183), 29 May 2026) [verified abstract only; scores not read]. SAE-targeted steering, which solves for vectors that hit chosen latents, reports a better strength/coherence trade-off than CAA [snippet] ([arXiv 2411.02193](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.02193v2), Nov 2024).

Detection does not imply a lever. Tiwari et al. decomposed a truth/deception probe on Gemma2-9B-IT into SAE latents ([arXiv 2609.18080](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18080), 16 Sep 2026) [verified abstract]. Latents ranked by probe alignment and by gradient sensitivity overlapped about 12% (Spearman 0.10). Ablating shared latents flipped outputs up to 27% of the time, probe-only latents 6%, random latents 1%. In Among Us, steering with the top SAE deception features did nothing at low strength and caused hallucination at high strength ([arXiv 2504.04072](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04072)). In Refusal Residue, steering along a detected alignment-faking direction barely changed compliance ([arXiv 2607.13346](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13346)). Treat detection and steering as separate claims, each needing its own test.

## Failure modes, with numbers

### Probes read the context, not only the concept

A probe fits whatever separates the labels in your dataset. Apollo's probe fired on honest answers in deception-themed scenarios and on honest confessions (a morality confound). Removing textual evidence of deception cut AUROC by 10 to 30 points in Boxo et al. With leakage-free splits, the Qwen3-32B alignment-faking probe fell to 0.43, and MLP probes overstated detectability by 0.2 to 0.3 AUROC (details in [deception_probes.md](../../research_notes/Broken%20promise%20probes%20related%20work/deception_probes.md) and [cot_vs_probes.md](../../research_notes/Broken%20promise%20probes%20related%20work/cot_vs_probes.md)). The sharpest 2026 result is label aliasing ([arXiv 2609.10739](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10739), Sep 2026). If a probe is fitted only on contexts where the true bit and the answer the task prescribes coincide, it cannot tell which one it measures. On a reward-trained Gemma-2-9B that always lies to rivals, a truth probe fitted on ally contexts scored **AUROC 0.006** on rivals, while probes fitted on mixed contexts scored 1.000 on the same activations. In instructed Llama-3.1-8B, two probes with ally accuracy 1.000 got rival AUROCs of 0.080 and 0.986. Marks's XOR post gives the mechanism: models linearly represent XORs of features, so a probe can learn `truth XOR topic` ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/hjJXCn9GsskysDceS/what-s-up-with-llms-representing-xors-of-arbitrary-features), via ARENA [unverified]). Controls in use: shuffled-label and random-label baselines at the same probe capacity (after Hewitt & Liang, [arXiv 1909.03368](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.03368)), held-out control data for thresholds, leakage-free splits, mixed-context fitting, and the MM-vs-LR causal check. Pooling is the other failure: mean pooling gave 99.13% FNR on long-context attacks in Gemini.

### SAE latents are one basis among many

SAEs reconstruct imperfectly, and the error matters. Part of SAE error is linearly predictable from the input. The rest does not shrink much with scale ([arXiv 2410.14670](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14670), Oct 2024) [not re-checked]. Splicing in OpenAI's 16M GPT-4 SAE gave loss comparable to a model trained with about 10× less compute ([arXiv 2406.04093](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04093)) [not re-checked]. Latents are not canonical. **Only 30% of latents were shared across seeds** for a 131K SAE on a Llama 3 8B MLP, and ReLU+L1 SAEs were more seed-stable than TopK ([arXiv 2501.16615](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16615), Jan 2025). Meta-SAEs split an "Einstein" latent into "scientist", "Germany" and "famous person", and larger SAEs contain latents smaller ones lack ([arXiv 2502.04878](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04878), ICLR 2025). Feature absorption makes a parent latent ("starts with S") fail on tokens that a child latent ("short") takes over, and changing width or sparsity does not fix it ([arXiv 2409.14507](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14507), Sep 2024). JumpReLU's L0 penalty produced dense latents firing on more than about 10% of tokens until GDM added a frequency penalty. Labels can mislead about function. Anthropic showed two CLT features with near-identical top activations that "have different causal effects on model behavior" ([May 2026 update](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/may-update/index.html)). GDM argues auto-interp scores should be weighted by latent frequency. SAEs also depend on their training distribution. Gemma Scope v1 trained on pretraining text lost more OOD than chat-fine-tuned SAEs, which is why GS2 fine-tunes its IT SAEs on chat rollouts. The GS2 open-problems list (Sep 2025) still includes feature splitting, dark matter and spurious compositions. Qwen-Scope adds one more limitation: with no published reconstruction metrics, you have to measure FVU and delta loss on your own data before trusting the 27B SAEs.

### Both can be trained around

Obfuscation attacks hit SAEs, probes and latent OOD detectors alike. Recall fell from 100% to 0% while a 90% jailbreak rate was kept ([arXiv 2412.09565](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09565), Dec 2024). RL against a fixed probe teaches evasion of token-level probes, while max-pool and attention probes held up ([arXiv 2506.14261](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.14261)). In Neural Chameleons, models fine-tuned to evade probes for 11 benign concepts generalised zero-shot to unseen deception monitors on Llama, Gemma and Qwen. That includes monitors trained after the fact on frozen weights. Capability cost was small, with the largest drop −7.6% on SocialIQA. Multi-layer, MLP and attention probes "mitigate but do not eliminate evasion" ([arXiv 2512.11949](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.11949), Dec 2025). Representation drift during RLVR causes obfuscation even without a probe penalty ([arXiv 2602.15515](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15515), ICML 2026). A model trained with rationalisations that frame hostility as protective evaded probes that caught an otherwise identical "liar" model 95%+ of the time ([arXiv 2603.25861](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25861), single author, no replication found). The counter-evidence is about how you train, not about the probe alone. GRPO against a lie detector produced over 85% deception, but high detector TPR or strong KL produced honest policies, and DPO stayed under 25% ([arXiv 2505.13787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13787)). Retraining probes after optimisation recovered accuracy ([arXiv 2510.21531](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.21531)). "Training against probes that do not update during training is an easily exploitable objective, while continuously updated probes substantially reduce harmfulness and improve honesty while preserving utility" ([arXiv 2609.38645](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38645), Sep 2026). GDM's production probes still had 22.97 to 42.46% FNR against adaptive red teaming ([arXiv 2601.11516](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11516)).

## Where the ARENA lab connects to SAEs

**1. Detection direction vs steering direction.** Section 3 shows MM gives a more causal direction than LR, quoting Geometry of Truth: MM outperformed LR and CCS "in 7/8 experimental conditions, often substantially". The lab is inconsistent about accuracy. The Section 4 intro says MM is more causal "despite similar accuracy", while the Section 3 objectives and the summary say "despite lower classification accuracy". The GoT abstract says MM "generalize[s] as well as other probing techniques". The accurate statement is similar or slightly lower accuracy, larger causal effect. The lab's reason, that LR overfits to correlational features, is the authors' gloss. GoT's own wording is softer: LR "can fail to reflect an intuitive best guess for the feature direction, even in the absence of confounding features" ([arXiv 2310.06824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06824)). The SAE link is a single parenthetical in the answer box to "Which probe type produces a more causally implicated direction?": "(Incidentally, this is also a motivator for why encoders and decoders in SAEs are untied - we use encoder vectors for detection but decoder vectors for steering.)" ([ARENA lab](https://github.com/callummcdougall/ARENA_3.0/blob/main/chapter1_transformer_interp/instructions/pages/11_%5B1.3.1%5D_Linear_Probes.md)). It is not a section or an exercise. The idea holds up in the SAE literature. GS2 initialises W_enc as the transpose of W_dec and then unties them. The encoder row has to cancel interference from other non-orthogonal latents, while the decoder column is the write direction. Absorption shows the split directly: an absorbed parent's encoder develops "holes" for child tokens while its decoder stays the same ([arXiv 2409.14507](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14507)). So an SAE gives each latent both a detector vector and a write vector. One limit applies: on AxBench, SAE decoder steering (0.165) still falls below a plain DiffMean vector (0.239).

**2. Generalisation.** The lab's cross-dataset matrix and deception section test whether one direction transfers to new data. An SAE latent does not depend on how you built a labelled dataset, which removes one source of bias. It does depend on the SAE's training data. Pretraining-text SAEs lose more OOD than chat-tuned ones, and GDM's view is that spurious correlations are represented by latents as readily as true signal ([Alignment Forum](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/4uXCAJNuPKtKBsi28/negative-results-for-saes-on-downstream-tasks)). Choosing the latent by its label costs about 0.25 AUROC on AxBench. You still have to check that the latent means what its label says, on your own data.

**3. Practical results.** Kantamneni et al. (14 of 113 wins, no ensemble gain), GDM (0.999 dense vs. a 0.048 OOD loss through the SAE) and AxBench (0.942 vs. 0.917 or 0.695) all find that a supervised probe on raw activations matches or beats an SAE-feature probe on a fixed detection task. SAEs earn their value in discovery and auditing. In Anthropic's auditing game, 3 of 4 blind teams found a hidden reward-model-sycophancy objective using SAEs, behavioural attacks and training-data search. The team that failed had API-only access ([arXiv 2503.10965](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10965), Mar 2025). In automated auditing, SAE tools raised agent win rates by about 10 to 20 points from near zero. They added less when the agent already had semantic search ([Anthropic, Jul 2025](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/automated-auditing/)). In Kantamneni et al.'s dataset debugging, an SAE latent suggested about 25% of CoLA labels were wrong, and another exposed a punctuation artifact in AI-vs-human text. In each case logistic regression found the same problems. Qwen-Scope's examples fit this pattern: benchmark redundancy, code-switching and repetition, all on Qwen3 models, all cases where nobody knew beforehand which concept to probe for.

**4. Training against the signal.** The lab warns that training against a probe can teach the model to hide what the probe measures. Bailey et al. show the same for SAEs (recall 100% to 0%). Neither method is safe as a frozen optimisation target. The 2026 evidence adds that continuously retrained probes are much harder to game, but no probe has been shown robust to a model trained specifically to hide from it.

## When to use which

| Situation | Use | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| You know the concept and can label examples (deception monitor, high-stakes or misuse filter) | Raw-activation probe: LR for monitoring, MM for causal checks, attention or rolling-max pooling over tokens | Higher accuracy, especially OOD; any model; minutes to train |
| You need to steer | Prompting first; then MM/CAA; SAE latents only with supervised selection | AxBench order: prompt 0.894 > LoRA 0.615 > DiffMean 0.239 > SAE 0.165 > probe 0.098 |
| You don't know what to look for, or need to find why a behaviour happens | SAE (Qwen-Scope on Qwen; Gemma Scope 2 on Gemma) | Discovery, auditing and dataset debugging are where SAEs added value |
| You have a probe and want to know what it reads | Both: decompose the probe direction into SAE latents, then test the candidates causally | Tiwari et al.: probe-aligned and causal latents overlap only about 12% |
| Deployment at scale | Probe as cheap first stage, stronger model behind it | Every published production system is a cascade |

The "SAE finds candidates, raw probe confirms" pipeline is used informally, as in Kantamneni et al.'s debugging cases and HypotheSAEs ([arXiv 2502.04382](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.04382v2)) [snippet]. The notes found no paper that benchmarks it as a method.

## What this means for this repo

The repo probes Qwen3.5-27B for broken promises in Warring States Diplomacy self-play. Three conclusions follow from the evidence above.

First, **the main detector should be a plain activation probe**: LR on raw residual activations at the promise, with MM fitted alongside, the cosine between the two reported, and multi-layer and pooled variants tried. Do not expect an SAE-latent probe to beat it, especially across games or opponents, which is the OOD case where SAEs lose most. An SAE-latent probe is still a cheap secondary baseline. The bar from the earlier report still applies: metadata alone reaches AUROC 0.76 on the Claude Opus data, so a probe result counts only as a gain over metadata on held-out games (Broken promise probes related work).

Second, **Qwen-Scope should be used to interpret what the probe picked up.** The match is unusually good. The 27B SAEs were trained on the same post-trained checkpoint the repo probes, so the base-to-instruct transfer problem that Riegler & Torpmann-Hagen flag for the other Qwen-Scope SAEs does not apply. The basic analysis is to unit-normalise the probe direction w (MM and LR separately) at layer L. Then compute its cosine with all 81,920 columns of W_dec, and with all rows of W_enc. Compare the top values with a null from random directions. In 5,120 dimensions a random direction has typical cosine near 1/√5120 ≈ 0.014 with any column, so a top cosine of, say, 0.3 means something. Then read the top-activating game messages for the top 20 to 50 latents and sort them into topic latents (war, betrayal, alliance vocabulary, a particular state's name) and latents closer to the plan (references to future orders, hedged commitments). Use the SAE ids that SAELens registers (`qwen-scope-3.5-27b-w80k-l50`, `layer{n}`), or load `layer{n}.sae.pt` directly and hook `model.model.layers[n]`. The SAELens path has not been run, and the effect of running the model in bf16 rather than the card's fp32 is untested. Before interpreting anything, measure FVU and the change in next-token loss when splicing the SAE in on your own game transcripts, because Qwen reports neither. Check the result at both k=50 and k=100. With 30% seed overlap in other SAEs, a conclusion that rests on one latent is weak. Neuronpedia's layer-31 source can help label latents only if its explanations exist, which is unconfirmed. Hooking a few layers is practical: each fp32 layer file is about 3.4 GB.

Third, **the topic-vs-intent question needs a behavioural control, not only an SAE reading.** The aliasing result is the template. A promise probe fitted where breaking happens mostly to rivals and keeping mostly to allies cannot tell "this is a rival" from "I will break this". Fit it on mixed contexts. Report AUROC separately within ally promises and within rival promises, and within matched partner, phase and threat context. The SAE analysis then becomes a check on that control. If the probe holds within ally and within rival subsets and its top decoder matches are not betrayal-topic latents, the intent reading is stronger. If its top matches are war or rival-name latents and within-context AUROC collapses, the probe reads the situation. The Fanatics result adds one more reason to probe the future action directly rather than a "deception" concept. An agent that rationalises a betrayal as strategy may never represent it as deceptive.

### Suggested experiment

The lab does not compare probe directions with SAE latents. A one-day version on this repo: run the lab's truth datasets (`cities`, `sp_en_trans`, `larger_than`) through Qwen3.5-27B with a chat template. Fit MM and LR at a middle layer, for example 31 to match Neuronpedia. Report the 3×3 transfer matrix as in the lab. Then rank Qwen-Scope latents by decoder cosine and encoder cosine with each direction, against the random-direction null. This checks three things before any promise data is involved: that the pipeline and SAE loading work, that MM aligns more with decoder columns than LR does (the untied-weights prediction), and whether a "truth" latent exists at all at that layer. A cheaper dry run is Qwen3.5-9B with its 64K Base SAE, at the cost of the base-to-instruct mismatch. Then repeat the same analysis on the broken-promise probe, with the ally/rival split.

## Conclusion

The question used to be "probe or SAE". The 2025 to 2026 evidence changes it to "probe, and SAE for explanation", with each tool assigned a separate job. The probe answers whether the model's state at the promise predicts the break. The SAE, plus the ally/rival control, answers what that predictive signal is. On Qwen3.5-27B, the second job is easier than on most models, because the SAEs match the probed checkpoint. It is also less validated, because Qwen published no reconstruction metrics and every Qwen-Scope result comes from other models. Two open points matter most for this repo. First, nobody has benchmarked the "SAE explains the probe" pipeline, so this project's analysis would count as a small contribution in its own right. Second, high decoding accuracy does not show causation (12% overlap in Tiwari et al.). A latent that explains the probe should therefore also be ablated, with a check on whether ablating it changes the agent's later orders.
